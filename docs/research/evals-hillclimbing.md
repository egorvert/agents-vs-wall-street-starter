# Evaluation & Hill-Climbing Design — Agents vs Wall Street

Research doc for the AI Tinkerers / OpenStocks / OpenAI / Primer hackathon, Sat 16 Aug 2026, London.
Written 15 Aug 2026. Scope: backtest dataset construction, leakage, metrics, small-n statistics, experiment loop, prior art.

**Headline finding up front:** because every current frontier model has a training cutoff on or before **May 2026**, and the **Q2-CY2026 earnings season ran ~13 July – 14 Aug 2026**, that entire season is *genuinely out-of-sample for every model we can call*. We do not need to solve the leakage problem in general — we need to build the dataset from the last 10 weeks. Everything else in this doc follows from that.

**The binding constraint is Claude Opus 5 at May 2026.** Any event whose *actual* was made public after **31 May 2026** is post-cutoff for every model in the table except Gemini-with-live-search.

Two important caveats:

- **Stated cutoff ≠ effective knowledge.** Shrivu Shankar's probing work finds Opus 5 "seems to know nothing more than previous Jan 2026 cutoff models" despite a published May 2026 date, and that knowledge decay near the cutoff is gradual rather than a cliff ([blog.sshh.io](https://blog.sshh.io/p/exploring-claudegpt-knowledge-cutoffs)). For leakage purposes this is the *safe* direction to be wrong in — but we should still use the **published** date as the boundary, never the probed one, because the probe is a lower bound on what the model knows.
- **Post-training / tool use re-introduces leakage.** If any model in the loop has web search enabled, the cutoff is irrelevant — it can just look up the answer. Retrieval must be through *our* tools, against *our* as-of-filtered corpus. This is the single easiest way to accidentally destroy the whole eval.

### 1.2 Documented evidence that this is a real problem

The literature is now unambiguous, and it is worth knowing the specific results because they tell us *which* mitigations actually work.

**Sarkar & Vafa (2024), "Lookahead Bias in Pretrained Language Models"** — [SSRN 4754678](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4754678) / [ICML 2025](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/51018) / [OpenReview PDF](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=fn9cJkB86T). Develops direct tests for lookahead bias premised on the assumption that some events are unpredictable from a prespecified information set. Finds lookahead bias in two applications, one of which is **predicting risk factors from corporate earnings calls** — i.e. exactly our input modality. Critically, they **discuss the limitations of prompting-based approaches** to counteract the bias. Telling a model "only use information available before date X" does not work.

**Lopez-Lira, Tang & Zhu (2025), "The Memorization Problem: Can We Trust LLMs' Economic Forecasts?"** — [arXiv 2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14765) / [SSRN 5217505](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5217505). The most quotable result: GPT-4o **recalls exact S&P 500 closing prices with <1% error for dates inside the training window**, and errors "explode" for post-cutoff dates. Their framing is the sharpest statement of the problem: under black-box access, counterfactual forecasting ability is **non-identified** when the model has seen the realized value — any output is consistent with both genuine skill and memorization. They also report that (a) instructions to respect historical boundaries fail to prevent recall-level accuracy, and (b) **masking fails, because LLMs reconstruct entities and dates from minimal context**. Their conclusion: the only practically feasible remedy is a hard training cutoff + rolling forward-only forecasts.

**Glasserman & Lin (2023/2024), "Assessing Look-Ahead Bias in Stock Return Predictions Generated by GPT Sentiment Analysis"** — [arXiv 2309.17322](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322) / [SSRN 4586726](https://ssrn.com/abstract=4586726) / [JFDS](https://www.pm-research.com/content/iijjfds/6/1/25). Separates two effects: **look-ahead bias** (model knows what followed) and a **distraction effect** (general knowledge of the named company interferes with the sentiment read). Anonymising company identifiers in headlines removed the bias — and actually *improved* performance by killing the distraction effect. Note this worked on short headlines; see below for why it fails on long documents.

**Gao, Jiang & Yan (2025/26), "A Test of Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts"** — [arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.23847) (CUHK). The most directly actionable paper for us. They define **Lookahead Propensity (LAP)**: send the model a *date-only recall query* — firm name, ticker, target date, and nothing else (no headline, no transcript, no realized value) — and read the first-token probabilities of `up` / `down` / `unknown`. LAP = P(up) + P(down). Because the query carries zero contemporaneous information, any non-trivial LAP must come from training. Their validation is the smoking gun: **LAP is materially positive throughout the in-sample period and collapses essentially to zero in the year immediately after the model's training cutoff.** Their Kodak example: on a date-only query for `2020-07-29` (day after the $765m DFC loan announcement, +318% move), Llama-3.3-70B assigns P(up) ≈ 0.9999 with `unknown` below 1e-5. They then show a positive interaction between LAP and the LLM forecast in an accuracy regression is a one-sided diagnostic for memorization contamination — and that the interaction **loses significance on post-cutoff samples**. This is the design we copy for our canary test.

**Wongchamcharoen & Glasserman (2025), "Do Large Language Models Understand Chronology?"** — [arXiv 2511.14214](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.14214). Tests whether prompt-based mitigation is even coherent: it presupposes the model understands "before 2016". Findings: exact-match ordering collapses as lists lengthen even while rank correlations stay high (local order preserved, global timeline not); single-prompt conditional sorting fails almost completely **at the filtering step**; explicit reasoning budget fixes it (GPT-5 at medium/high effort achieves flawless ordering and perfect conditional sorting, low/minimal degrades). Their conclusion: "this finding does not bode well for prompt-based mitigation of look-ahead bias." Practical read for us: **if we do use any prompt-level temporal constraint, run the model at high reasoning effort** — but do not rely on it as the primary control.

**Li et al. (2025), "Profit Mirage: Revisiting Information Leakage in LLM-based Financial Agents"** — [arXiv 2510.07920](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07920). Introduces FinLake-Bench and the FactFin framework. The headline: **most published LLM-based financial agents fail to beat baselines once their knowledge cutoff is passed.** They demonstrate it by comparing a historical trade period (Q2–Q3 2021) with a latest period (Q3–Q4 2024). This is the paper to quote at the judges if we want to explain why our numbers look modest compared to whatever the team next to us is claiming.

**Roy & Roy (2026), "MemGuard-Alpha"** — [arXiv 2603.26797](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.26797). Two useful things. (1) A clean quantification of the contamination/accuracy crossover: as contamination quintile rises Q1→Q5, **in-sample accuracy rises 40.8% → 52.5% while out-of-sample accuracy falls 47% → 42%**. Memorization inflates apparent accuracy at the direct cost of generalization. (2) **Cross-Model Memorization Disagreement (CMMD)**: exploit the natural variation in training cutoffs across models to separate memorization from reasoning. Cheap for us — we already have models at Jul 2025, Aug 2025, Jan 2026, Feb 2026, and May 2026 cutoffs.

**Merchant & Levy (2025), "A Fast and Effective Solution to the Problem of Look-ahead Bias in LLMs"** — [arXiv 2512.06607](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06607) (Chicago Booth, NeurIPS 2025 GenAI-in-Finance workshop). Inference-time unlearning: adjust the frontier model's logits using the difference between two small fine-tuned models (one on the to-forget corpus, one on the to-retain corpus). Removes verbatim *and* semantic knowledge; even trigram LMs work as the auxiliaries. **Not feasible in a one-day hackathon** (needs logit access, which the Anthropic/OpenAI APIs do not give), but worth naming in the writeup as the state of the art we're approximating with a date filter.

**Benhenda (2026), "Look-Ahead-Bench"** — [arXiv 2601.13770](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.13770), [code](https://github.com/benstaf/lookaheadbench). Defines **alpha decay** — the performance drop between in-sample and out-of-sample windows — as the bias metric. Standard LLMs show alpha decay exceeding **−15 percentage points**; purpose-built point-in-time models don't. Good framing: *the gap between your in-sample and out-of-sample score IS the leakage estimate.* We can compute this for free and it makes a great slide.

### 1.3 Mitigation strategies, ranked by what actually works

| # | Strategy | Effectiveness | Cost to us | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **a** | **Evaluate only on post-cutoff quarters** | Eliminates by construction. Gao et al. show LAP collapses to ~0 immediately after cutoff. | Free — the data exists. | **PRIMARY. Do this.** |
| **c** | **Canary / LAP test to measure memorization per event** | Directly measures it; validated by cutoff-collapse. | ~1 cheap call per event. | **DO — as the gate on any pre-cutoff event we add.** |
| **d** | **Restrict retrieval to point-in-time documents** | Necessary but not sufficient (parametric leakage remains). Also the thing most likely to be got wrong silently. | Medium — needs disciplined as-of filtering. | **DO — non-negotiable regardless.** |
| **b** | **Entity anonymisation** (Kim/Muhn/Nikolaev; Glasserman/Lin) | Works on *short* text (headlines). **Fails on long documents** — Sarkar & Vafa and Lopez-Lira et al. both find models see through anonymisation and reconstruct the entity. | High (must scrub tickers, exec names, segment names, product names, dollar magnitudes…). | **SKIP as a leakage control.** Consider as an *ablation* only. |
| — | Prompt-based temporal instruction ("only use info before X") | Documented to fail (Sarkar & Vafa; Lopez-Lira et al.; Glasserman chronology paper). | Free. | Include as belt-and-braces, **claim nothing from it**. |
| — | Cross-model disagreement (CMMD) | Good secondary signal. | 1 extra cheap call per event. | Nice-to-have if time allows. |
| — | Inference-time unlearning / PiT-trained models | State of the art. | Needs logit access / model training. | **Out of scope.** |

Note on (b): Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev's anonymisation ([arXiv 2407.17866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866)) was designed to test whether the *model's financial-analysis skill* was real, in a setting where the input was a **standardised balance sheet and income statement with no names, no dates, no narrative** — a much stronger scrub than masking a ticker in a transcript. Their conclusion that "LLM prediction does not stem from its training memory" is credible *for that input format*. It does not license anonymising a transcript and calling it clean.

### 1.4 Recommended protocol for a one-day hackathon

**Tier A — the clean set (build this first, it is the whole ballgame).**

All US earnings announcements with **announcement datetime > 2026-05-31**. As of today that is essentially the complete Q2-CY2026 season: roughly **13 July – 14 Aug 2026**, giving ~490 S&P 500 events plus several thousand smaller names. Post-cutoff for every model in §1.1. No canary test needed. No anonymisation needed. Prompt-level date instructions become cosmetic.

One honest caveat to state in the writeup: for a June-quarter-end, the *quarter itself* (Apr–Jun 2026) partly precedes Opus 5's May 2026 cutoff. That is not leakage of the answer — the answer was published in July — but it does mean the model has genuine parametric knowledge of April/May context. This makes a "with vs without retrieval" ablation confounded on Tier A. Say so; don't hide it.

**Tier B — the extension set (only if Tier A is exhausted).**

Announcements between **2026-02-01 and 2026-05-31** (Q4-2025 and Q1-2026 seasons). Post-cutoff for Sonnet 5 / Opus 4.8 / GPT-5.5 / GPT-5.6 (Jan–Feb 2026), **pre-cutoff for Opus 5**. Every Tier-B event must pass the canary test below before it enters the eval set, and must be tagged so we can report Tier A and Tier B separately.

**Tier C — pre-2026.** Do not use for scoring. Use only as a *development smoke test* for pipeline plumbing, clearly labelled as contaminated.

**The canary test (implement this — 30 lines).**

Per candidate event, with **no tools, no retrieval, temperature default**, using structured output:

```
System: You are being audited for factual recall. Answer only from memory.
User:   For {COMPANY} ({TICKER}), fiscal {QUARTER}: what were the reported
        total revenue and diluted EPS? If you do not know, set known=false.

Schema: { known: boolean, revenue_usd: number|null, eps: number|null,
          confidence: number }   // confidence in [0,1]
```

- Sample `N=3`. Compute `LAP = fraction of samples with known=true` (this is Gao et al.'s P(known) with an abstain option, adapted to an API that doesn't expose logits).
- **Exclude the event if** any sample gives revenue within **±2%** of actual, **or** EPS within **±3 cents** of actual, **or** `LAP ≥ 0.67`.
- **Calibrate the false-positive rate** by running the identical canary over a sample of known-clean Tier-A events. If Tier-A LAP is ~0 and Tier-B LAP is materially positive, that reproduces the cutoff-collapse result and is itself a slide-worthy chart.
- Log LAP per event *even for Tier A*, and report `win_rate | LAP=0` vs `win_rate | LAP>0` as our alpha-decay diagnostic.

**Hard rules for the run harness:**

1. **Web search / native browsing OFF** on every model call. All retrieval goes through our own tools.
2. Every tool that returns a document takes a mandatory `as_of` parameter and filters on it server-side inside our code, not in the prompt.
3. The as-of boundary is the **filing acceptance datetime minus 1 second** of the earnings 8-K, not the calendar date. (Post-close releases at 16:05 ET on day *T* mean day-*T* news is fair game; a date-granular filter leaks or over-censors.)
4. Store the model ID and its cutoff in the run manifest. If someone swaps the model mid-day, the eval set's validity changes and we need to know.

## 2. Point-in-time data discipline

### 2.1 Constructing the as-of snapshot

For each event, freeze an `as_of` timestamp = **acceptance datetime of the earnings 8-K − ε**. Everything the agent may see must be provably dated before it.

| Layer | Source | As-of key |
|---|---|---|
| Prior filings (10-Q/10-K) | SEC EDGAR | acceptance datetime from the daily index |
| Prior earnings 8-K + EX-99.1 press releases | SEC EDGAR, form=8-K, Item 2.02 | acceptance datetime |
| XBRL fundamentals | `data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyconcept` / `companyfacts` | filed date on each fact — **use the fact's own `filed`/`accn`, never the latest restated value** |
| Transcripts | see §2.3 | call date |
| News | see §2.4 | published_at |
| Prices | daily bars | trade date < as_of date |
| Consensus | see §2.2 | **the hard one** |

**SEC EDGAR specifics** ([Accessing EDGAR Data](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-search-assistance/accessing-edgar-data), [Developer Resources](https://www.sec.gov/about/developer-resources)):

- Rate limit **10 requests/second per IP**, enforced as a sustained rate; short bursts tolerated, sustained excess → HTTP 403 for ~10 min. Descriptive `User-Agent` with a real contact email is required.
- Full-text search: `https://efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index?q=...&forms=8-K&dateRange=custom&startdt=...&enddt=...&ciks=...`, JSON, `size` max 100, paginate with `from`. Undocumented and stricter-throttled than the document servers. No API key. ([EFTS parameter guide](https://tldrfiling.com/blog/sec-edgar-full-text-search-api), [rate-limit notes](https://edgarkit.com/learn/sec-edgar-rate-limits))
- **Acceptance datetime**: this is the field that matters and it is not in the plain master index. Get it from `https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK##########.json`, which carries a parallel `acceptanceDateTime` array alongside `accessionNumber` / `filingDate` / `form` / `primaryDocument`. Alternatively parse the `<ACCEPTANCE-DATETIME>` header in the raw `.txt` submission at `/Archives/edgar/data/{cik}/{accession}.txt`. **Filing date alone is not enough** — a 16:05 ET release and an 08:00 ET release on the same day have very different information sets.
- Indexes: `/Archives/edgar/daily-index/`, `/Archives/edgar/full-index/`. Daily indexes are built nightly from ~22:00 ET.
- Bulk: `companyfacts.zip` and `submissions.zip` are single large downloads — grab these once at the start of the day rather than hammering the API 490 times.

**Cache everything to disk on first fetch, keyed by (source, symbol, as_of).** The eval will be re-run 20+ times; the network must be hit once.

### 2.2 Historical consensus — the genuinely hard part

**Verified position:** cheap APIs will sell you *current* forward estimates readily. Getting the consensus **as it stood the day before a past announcement** is where they all get vague, and where the leakage risk hides.

| **FMP** `stable/earnings-calendar` | Returns past + upcoming announcements with `epsEstimated` and `epsActual`. **This is the practical path** — the `epsEstimated` on a past row is the estimate carried at announcement. **Recommendation:** buy **one month of FMP Premium ($59)** tonight for the earnings calendar + estimates, and file the **Estimize free trial / historical-testing-file request** in parallel — Estimize is the only source in this list that *advertises* point-in-time consensus time series, and their trial is free. Do not block on Estimize; FMP is the fallback.

**The restatement gotcha, stated plainly:** if a provider refreshes its `estimate` field retroactively (e.g. re-derives it from a later vintage of the analyst panel), that field contains future information and using it as "the consensus we had to beat" is itself a form of leakage — it flatters or penalises us with data nobody had. **Detection:** snapshot the consensus for a handful of *upcoming* Q3-2026 events today, re-pull the same rows tomorrow and Saturday morning, and diff. If a past-quarter row ever changes, the field is not frozen and we must say so in the writeup.

**Proxy when historical consensus is unavailable.** Earnings-surprise endpoints return `(actual, estimate, surprise, surprisePercent)` for past quarters. The consensus is recoverable as `estimate = actual − surprise`, or `estimate = actual / (1 + surprisePercent)`. Two caveats:
1. This is the consensus **at announcement**, which is what we want (that is the number the market traded against and the number the hackathon almost certainly scores on).
2. It is *one* number, not a time series — we cannot ask "what was consensus 30 days out?" That's fine for our purposes.

If even that is missing for a name, the **fallback benchmark is a seasonal random walk** (`E[Q_t] = Q_{t-4} + δ`, the classic SUE expectation — [Wikipedia: earnings surprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earnings_surprise)). Report it as "vs SRW baseline" and label it as such — it is not consensus, and beating it is much easier.

### 2.3 Transcripts

- **FMP Ultimate ($149/mo)** includes earnings call transcripts. Overkill for one day.
- **Finnhub**: 15+ years of transcripts, but *not* included even in the All-In-One plan (contact sales). Dead end for tomorrow.
- **Alpha Vantage** `EARNINGS_CALL_TRANSCRIPT` — free tier exists but very low daily limits.
- **Pragmatic path:** we do not actually need the *target quarter's* transcript (it doesn't exist yet at forecast time). We need the **prior** quarter's transcript, which is where guidance lives. Two or three prior transcripts per company is a small enough set to scrape from Motley Fool / Seeking Alpha for a 40–60 name dev set.

### 2.4 News with trustworthy timestamps

The requirement is a **strict `published_before` filter that actually holds**. Ranked by how much I'd trust the date:

1. **SEC filings themselves** — the timestamp is authoritative. Prefer these over news wherever possible.
2. **GDELT** — free, every record carries a crawl timestamp, queryable by date window. Noisy but honest about dates.
3. **Finnhub `/company-news`** (free tier, `from`/`to` params) and **Tiingo news** — explicit date ranges; reasonably trustworthy.
4. **Exa / Brave / Serper with date filters** — convenient, but **these leak**. A date-filtered search engine filters on the *document's claimed publish date*, which is frequently wrong (re-published articles, updated pages, "last modified" dates, aggregator reprints), and snippets are generated by an index built *today*. **Do not use a general web-search tool as the agent's news source in the backtest.** Use them for our own research, not the agent's.

Rule: any news item whose `published_at` we cannot verify from a first-party source is excluded from the agent's context. When in doubt, drop it — a smaller clean corpus beats a larger leaky one.

### 2.5 Prices

Only needed for scaling metrics (§3) and for the price-as-of on the forecast date. Any daily-bar source is fine; use the close on `as_of − 1 trading day`. Watch survivorship: a symbol that delisted between the event and today may be missing. Tier A is 10 weeks old, so this is a non-issue for us.

## 3. Metrics

### 3.1 What consensus error actually looks like (the bar we must clear)

From [FactSet Earnings Insight, S&P 500 update 24 July 2026](https://insight.factset.com/sp-500-earnings-season-update-july-24-2026):

| | Q2 2026 | 5-yr avg | 10-yr avg |
|---|---|---|---|
| % of S&P 500 reporting EPS **above** estimates | 86% | **78%** | **76%** |
| Aggregate earnings vs estimates | +39.3% | **+7.0%** | **+7.4%** |
| % reporting revenue **above** estimates | 80% | **70%** | **68%** |
| Aggregate revenue vs estimates | +2.8% | **+1.9%** | **+1.6%** |

Two design-critical readings:

**(i) Revenue is a ~2% problem; EPS is a ~7% problem.** Aggregate revenue lands ~1.6–1.9% above consensus on average; aggregate earnings ~7%. Revenue is far more forecastable and the errors are far better behaved. **Expect our edge, if any, to come from revenue.**

**(ii) Consensus is systematically biased low.** 76–78% of companies beat EPS; 68–70% beat revenue. That is not noise, it's the well-documented walk-down of guidance. It has an immediate consequence for the scoring rule.

### 3.2 The must-beat baseline (do not skip this)

Because `actual > consensus` roughly 76% of the time on EPS and 69% on revenue, a **constant positive offset** — `forecast = consensus × (1 + k)` — beats consensus on |error| whenever the realised surprise exceeds `k/2`. Fitting `k` to the historical median surprise gives a strategy with **no model, no retrieval, and no intelligence** that wins well over 50% of paired comparisons.

**Implement this baseline in the first hour and put it on the leaderboard.** Three reasons:
1. If our agent cannot beat it, we have learned something important before lunch.
2. It is the honest floor for "did the LLM add anything?"
3. If the hackathon rubric is naive, it may well be a medal-winning submission on its own — and we should know that rather than discover it at 6pm.

Baselines to implement, in order of effort:
- `B0` **Consensus itself** (win-rate 0.5 by construction; sanity check the harness).
- `B1` **Consensus × (1 + k)**, k fitted on Tier-B / pre-2026 data only.
- `B2` **Seasonal random walk**: `Q_{t-4}` scaled by trailing YoY growth.
- `B3` **Trend + guidance**: prior-quarter company guidance midpoint where available, else B2.

### 3.3 Revenue metric

Revenue is strictly positive and large; percentage errors are well behaved.

- **Primary:** `APE_rev = |forecast − actual| / actual`. Report the **median**, not the mean — the distribution has a long right tail from small caps and one-off acquisitions.
- Report **MAPE too**, winsorised at the 1st/99th percentile, so the number is comparable to whatever the judges quote.
- **sMAPE is unnecessary here.** sMAPE's only advantage is bounded behaviour when the denominator can approach zero; revenue never does. Adding it buys nothing and costs interpretability.

### 3.4 EPS metric — why percentage errors break, and what to use instead

**The problem, concretely.** EPS crosses zero. If actual EPS = $0.02, consensus = $0.05, ours = $0.04: consensus APE = 150%, ours = 100%. A **3-cent miss** is rendered as a 150% error. If actual EPS = $0.00 exactly, APE is undefined. Roughly 10–15% of quarters in a broad universe sit inside ±$0.10, and loss-making quarters flip the sign of the denominator. **Any mean-of-percentages EPS metric is dominated by the handful of near-zero names, which is exactly backwards** — those are the least economically meaningful events.

sMAPE does not rescue this. sMAPE = `2|F−A| / (|F|+|A|)` is bounded at 200%, but it **saturates**: once forecast and actual straddle zero, sMAPE pins near its maximum for a 1-cent miss and a 50-cent miss alike. It converts a scale problem into a ceiling problem.

**The empirical fact that settles the design.** Cheong & Thomas (2010), ["Why do EPS forecast error and dispersion not vary with scale?"](https://raw.rutgers.edu/docs/seminars/Cheong_Thomas_2010.pdf), sort firms into share-price deciles and find that **absolute consensus forecast error is essentially constant in cents across the entire price range**: median |FE| of **2–3 cents**, mean **5–6 cents**, interquartile range **4 or 5 cents**, for *all ten deciles*. Median analyst dispersion is **2 cents** in every decile. Their explicit warning: *"deflating by share price or actual/forecast EPS creates a strong negative relation between scale and deflated variability… inferences from prior research based on deflated measures need to be reevaluated."*

That is decisive. The natural unit of EPS forecast error is **cents**, not percent.

**Recommended EPS metric stack:**

| Rank | Metric | Definition | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **Median absolute error in cents** | `median(|F − A|) × 100` | Cheong–Thomas: the error is scale-free in cents. Directly comparable to the 2–3¢ consensus median. |
| Secondary | **Price-scaled AE** | `|F − A| / P_{as_of} × 100` (%) | The standard accounting-literature deflator; makes cross-sectional comparison legitimate. Gleason et al. report mean AFE/price of **1.16%** for annual forecasts ([PDF](https://care-mendoza.nd.edu/assets/152422/gleason_6_06.pdf)). |
| Secondary | **Revenue-scaled AE** | `|F − A| × shares / revenue` | Ties EPS error to business scale; robust when price is volatile. |
| Diagnostic | **SUE-style** | `(A − F) / σ(analyst forecasts)` | Only if we get dispersion from Estimize. Bakx's [comparison of five scaling methods](http://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=129641) finds SUE the best proxy for earnings surprise — though the differences between methods are small, and **unscaled error came second, price-scaling came last**. |
| **Banned** | MAPE / mean APE on EPS | — | Undefined at zero, explodes near zero, dominated by irrelevant names. |
| **Banned** | sMAPE on EPS | — | Saturates at the ceiling once signs differ. |

Also worth knowing: Bartov's ["Robust Measures of Earnings Surprises"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.12746) (JF) argues the standard consensus error `CE` is a poor approximation of the ideal bias-adjusted measure, and that the **fraction of individual forecasts missed on the same side (FOM)** — which discards magnitude entirely — is far better at predicting returns. Interesting; not our primary metric, but it supports the general point that magnitude-based EPS error measures are fragile.

### 3.5 The head-to-head metric (this is what the hackathon actually scores)

The brief says the comparison is `|our error| < |consensus error|` per event, plus margin. So make that *the* metric, not an afterthought.

**Binary — Paired Win Rate (PWR).** Per event *e* and target *t* ∈ {revenue, EPS}:

```
win(e,t) = 1    if |err_ours| < |err_cons| − τ
         = 0.5  if ||err_ours| − |err_cons|| ≤ τ      (tie band)
         = 0    otherwise
PWR(t) = mean over events of win(e,t)
```

Set the tie band `τ` to a real economic threshold, not zero: **0.5 cents for EPS, 0.1% for revenue.** Without it, floating-point-scale "wins" pollute the count and the metric rewards nothing.

**Continuous — Relative Skill Score (RSS).** Bounded, symmetric, and tie-centred at zero:

```
RSS(e,t) = ( |err_cons| − |err_ours| ) / ( |err_cons| + |err_ours| )     ∈ [−1, +1]
```

`+1` = we were exact; `−1` = consensus was exact; `0` = tied. The bounded range is the point: a single catastrophic miss on one illiquid name cannot dominate the aggregate the way a raw ratio would. Report `mean RSS` and `median RSS`.

**Composite leaderboard score.** Equal weight, because revenue-only skill is not a finished product:

```
SCORE = 0.5 × RSS(revenue) + 0.5 × RSS(EPS)
```

**Guardrail metrics — every run must emit all of these:**

| Guardrail | Why it exists |
|---|---|
| `coverage` — % of events producing a parseable forecast | A config that "wins" by refusing hard names is not winning. **Score refusals as losses**, and track the rate separately. |
| `directional accuracy` — `sign(F − consensus) == sign(A − consensus)` | The beat/miss call. Robust, tradeable, and immune to every scaling problem above. |
| `bias` — mean signed `(F − A)`, in cents and in % | Detects the model inheriting the analyst walk-down, or overcorrecting for it. |
| `interval coverage` — if we emit an 80% interval, its empirical hit rate | Calibration. Cheap credibility with judges. |
| `LAP / contamination flag` per event | §1.4. Also lets us report `PWR | clean` vs `PWR | contaminated` — our alpha-decay number. |
| `cost_usd`, `latency_s`, `tool_calls` per event | We will be optimising these by 4pm. |
| `PWR by sector / by market-cap decile` | Where the edge lives. Almost certainly not uniform. |

## 4. Statistical reality with n = 10–40

This is the section most hackathon teams skip and it is the one that determines whether our conclusions are real. **I computed these exactly rather than citing rules of thumb.**

### 4.1 Sign test power — the brutal table

One-sided exact binomial (sign) test, α = 0.05, H₀: p = 0.5.

| n | wins needed to reject | as % | power @ p=0.60 | 0.65 | 0.70 | 0.75 | 0.80 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 | 9 | 90% | 0.05 | 0.09 | 0.15 | 0.24 | 0.38 |
| 15 | 12 | 80% | 0.09 | 0.17 | 0.30 | 0.46 | 0.65 |
| **20** | **15** | **75%** | 0.13 | 0.25 | 0.42 | 0.62 | 0.80 |
| 25 | 18 | 72% | 0.15 | 0.31 | 0.51 | 0.73 | 0.89 |
| **30** | **20** | **67%** | 0.29 | 0.51 | 0.73 | 0.89 | 0.97 |
| **40** | **26** | **65%** | 0.32 | 0.57 | 0.81 | 0.95 | 0.99 |
| 60 | 37 | 62% | 0.45 | 0.75 | 0.94 | 0.99 | 1.00 |
| 100 | 59 | 59% | 0.62 | 0.91 | 0.99 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

Minimum n for 80% power (one-sided, α=0.05): **p=0.60 → n=158**; p=0.65 → **n=69**; p=0.70 → **n=37**; p=0.75 → n=23; p=0.80 → n=18.

**Read this as:** at n=20 we need **15 of 20** to claim significance, and if our true skill is a genuinely-good 65% we will detect it only **25%** of the time. At n=40, 26/40. **A 60% win-rate is not detectable at any n we can afford** — so stop treating 60% as a target and treat it as noise.

95% Wilson CI half-width around an observed 50%: n=10 → ±0.26; n=20 → ±0.20; n=30 → ±0.17; n=40 → ±0.15; n=60 → ±0.12; n=100 → ±0.10. **At n=30 our confidence interval is 34 points wide.** Any config difference smaller than that is invisible.

### 4.2 The winner's curse — why a held-out set is mandatory

Expected **best observed** win-rate across *k* configs when **all of them are truly 50%**:

| n | k=1 | k=5 | k=10 | k=20 | k=50 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 | 0.500 | 0.629 | 0.670 | 0.706 | **0.746** |
| 30 | 0.500 | 0.606 | 0.640 | 0.669 | 0.703 |
| 40 | 0.500 | 0.592 | 0.621 | 0.647 | 0.676 |
| 60 | 0.500 | 0.575 | 0.599 | 0.620 | 0.644 |

P(at least one of *k* configs reaches ≥65% win-rate | all truly 50%):

| n | k=1 | k=10 | k=20 | k=50 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 | 0.13 | 0.76 | **0.94** | 1.00 |
| 30 | 0.05 | 0.40 | 0.64 | 0.92 |
| 40 | 0.04 | 0.34 | 0.56 | 0.87 |
| 60 | 0.01 | 0.13 | 0.24 | 0.50 |

**If we try 20 prompt variants against a 20-event dev set, we are 94% likely to find one that "wins 65%+" purely by chance.** That is the entire hackathon failure mode in one number. It is also roughly what a normal hill-climbing day looks like — 20 configs is not an unusual count, it's Tuesday.

### 4.3 Practical statistical protocol

1. **Split the data before writing any prompt.** Randomly, stratified by sector and market-cap tercile:
   - **DEV = 40 events.** Iterate freely. Never report a DEV number as a result.
   - **TEST = 150+ events.** Touch **exactly twice**: a mid-day checkpoint (~14:00) and the final run (~18:00). Log both. If we touch it a third time, it is a dev set.
   - Fix the split with a seed and commit it to the repo before the first experiment.
2. **Rank on effect size, gate on statistics.** Use `mean RSS` for ranking configs (continuous → far more power per event than a binary), and the sign test / bootstrap only as a "are we fooling ourselves" gate at the end.
3. **Paired everything.** Every config sees the identical event list, and we compare *per-event differences*. Paired comparisons remove event difficulty, which is by far the largest variance component.
4. **Bootstrap CIs, not Wilcoxon.** Wilcoxon signed-rank is more powerful than the sign test because it uses magnitude, but it assumes the differences are symmetrically distributed — and paired |error| differences are violently right-skewed. Use a **paired BCa bootstrap over events, 10,000 resamples**, resampling *events* (not event-target pairs, which are correlated within a company). Report the CI on `mean RSS` and on `PWR`.
5. **Pre-register the primary metric** in the README at 09:30 and do not change it. Changing the metric after seeing results is the same error as changing the hypothesis.
6. **Report the number of configs tried** alongside the winning score. If we tried 20 and the best hit 68% on 40 events, the honest statement is "68%, best of 20, where chance alone gives 65%."
7. **Cheap variance reduction that actually works:** more events beats more clever statistics. Going from 20 → 60 events cuts the CI half-width from ±0.20 to ±0.12 and takes ~20 extra minutes of compute. **Prefer spending the day's budget on eval-set size over prompt variants.**

## 5. Hill-climbing workflow mechanics

### 5.1 SQLite schema

Six tables. This is the whole experiment system.

```sql
-- Immutable event universe. Built once, in the morning.
CREATE TABLE events (
  event_id            TEXT PRIMARY KEY,      -- {ticker}:{fiscal_period}
  ticker              TEXT NOT NULL,
  cik                 TEXT,
  company_name        TEXT,
  fiscal_period       TEXT NOT NULL,         -- '2026Q2'
  period_end_date     TEXT NOT NULL,
  announce_datetime   TEXT NOT NULL,         -- 8-K acceptance datetime, ISO8601 UTC
  as_of               TEXT NOT NULL,         -- announce_datetime - 1s; THE cutoff
  actual_revenue      REAL,
  actual_eps          REAL,
  consensus_revenue   REAL,
  consensus_eps       REAL,
  consensus_source    TEXT,                  -- 'fmp_calendar' | 'estimize' | 'derived_surprise'
  n_estimates         INTEGER,
  eps_dispersion      REAL,
  price_as_of         REAL,
  shares_diluted      REAL,
  sector              TEXT,
  mktcap_bucket       TEXT,
  tier                TEXT NOT NULL,         -- 'A' post-2026-05-31 | 'B' | 'C'
  split               TEXT NOT NULL,         -- 'dev' | 'test'
  lap_score           REAL,                  -- canary: fraction of samples claiming knowledge
  contaminated        INTEGER DEFAULT 0
);

-- Every config is content-addressed. Never mutate; always insert a new row.
CREATE TABLE configs (
  config_id       TEXT PRIMARY KEY,          -- sha256 of the canonical JSON below
  created_at      TEXT NOT NULL,
  label           TEXT,                      -- human name: 'v7-guidance-first'
  parent_id       TEXT,                      -- what we forked from -> lineage tree
  model           TEXT NOT NULL,
  model_cutoff    TEXT NOT NULL,             -- validity of the eval depends on this
  effort          TEXT,
  system_prompt   TEXT NOT NULL,
  tools_json      TEXT NOT NULL,
  params_json     TEXT NOT NULL,             -- k-shot, retrieval depth, ensemble n, ...
  notes           TEXT
);

-- One row per eval invocation.
CREATE TABLE runs (
  run_id        TEXT PRIMARY KEY,
  config_id     TEXT NOT NULL REFERENCES configs(config_id),
  started_at    TEXT NOT NULL,
  finished_at   TEXT,
  split         TEXT NOT NULL,               -- 'dev' | 'test'
  tier_filter   TEXT,
  n_events      INTEGER,
  git_sha       TEXT NOT NULL,
  dataset_hash  TEXT NOT NULL,               -- hash of the event_id list -> comparability
  status        TEXT                         -- 'running'|'ok'|'failed'
);

-- One row per (run, event). The raw evidence.
CREATE TABLE predictions (
  run_id            TEXT NOT NULL REFERENCES runs(run_id),
  event_id          TEXT NOT NULL REFERENCES events(event_id),
  pred_revenue      REAL,
  pred_eps          REAL,
  rev_low  REAL, rev_high REAL,              -- 80% interval
  eps_low  REAL, eps_high REAL,
  rationale         TEXT,
  raw_response      TEXT,
  parse_ok          INTEGER NOT NULL,
  n_tool_calls      INTEGER,
  input_tokens      INTEGER,
  output_tokens     INTEGER,
  cached_tokens     INTEGER,
  cost_usd          REAL,
  latency_ms        INTEGER,
  error             TEXT,
  PRIMARY KEY (run_id, event_id)
);

-- Denormalised scores. Recomputable from predictions; kept for fast leaderboards.
CREATE TABLE scores (
  run_id            TEXT PRIMARY KEY REFERENCES runs(run_id),
  n_scored          INTEGER, coverage REAL,
  pwr_revenue REAL, pwr_eps REAL, pwr_combined REAL,
  rss_revenue REAL, rss_eps REAL, score_composite REAL,
  rss_rev_ci_lo REAL, rss_rev_ci_hi REAL,   -- BCa bootstrap
  rss_eps_ci_lo REAL, rss_eps_ci_hi REAL,
  median_ape_revenue REAL,
  median_abs_eps_cents REAL,
  median_eps_ae_over_price REAL,
  dir_acc_revenue REAL, dir_acc_eps REAL,
  bias_revenue_pct REAL, bias_eps_cents REAL,
  sign_test_p_rev REAL, sign_test_p_eps REAL,
  total_cost_usd REAL, wall_clock_s REAL
);

-- Content-addressed fetch cache. THE most important table for iteration speed.
CREATE TABLE doc_cache (
  cache_key    TEXT PRIMARY KEY,             -- sha256(source|ticker|doc_type|as_of|params)
  source       TEXT, ticker TEXT, doc_type TEXT,
  as_of        TEXT NOT NULL,
  published_at TEXT NOT NULL,                -- MUST be < as_of; assert on read
  content      TEXT,
  fetched_at   TEXT NOT NULL,
  bytes        INTEGER
);
CREATE INDEX idx_cache_lookup ON doc_cache(ticker, doc_type, as_of);
```

Two invariants worth enforcing in code, not comments:
- `doc_cache` reads **assert `published_at < as_of`** and throw otherwise. This turns a silent leak into a crash.
- `configs.config_id` = sha256 of `(model, effort, system_prompt, tools_json, params_json)`. Identical configs collapse to one row automatically, so "did we already try this?" is a lookup, not a memory exercise.

### 5.2 Caching — the thing that makes 20 iterations possible

Three layers:

1. **Document cache** (`doc_cache` above). Fetch each (ticker, doc_type, as_of) exactly once. After the first run of the day, the eval does **zero network I/O** outside the model API. This alone takes a run from ~15 min to ~4 min.
2. **Prompt caching on the model side.** Put the frozen system prompt + tool definitions first, volatile per-event content last, and set a cache breakpoint on the last stable block. On Claude, cache reads are ~0.1× input price. **~65% cost reduction** in the table below. Watch for silent invalidators: no `Date.now()`, no UUIDs, no unsorted JSON in the prefix.
3. **Prediction memoisation.** Key on `(config_id, event_id)`. Re-running a config after adding 10 events should only cost the 10 new events. This makes "grow the eval set" nearly free, which is exactly the behaviour §4.3 says we want to encourage.

### 5.3 Parallelism

- Event-level fan-out with a bounded worker pool. **Start at concurrency 8**, raise until 429s appear, then back off 25%.
- At ~60 s/event and concurrency 8: **DEV (40 events) ≈ 5 minutes. TEST (150 events) ≈ 19 minutes.**
- Five minutes is the right dev-loop cadence — long enough to think, short enough to stay in flow.
- Retry with exponential backoff on 429/5xx. Cap retries at 3, then record `parse_ok=0` and **score it as a loss**. Never let a config quietly improve its score by dropping hard events.
- Keep a `--limit N --seed S` flag for 10-event smoke tests during plumbing work.

Assumes ~60k input tokens/event (filings excerpts + prior transcript + news) and ~3k output tokens.

## 6. Prior art

### 6.1 Crowdsourced forecasts — Estimize (the target to aim at)

**Estimize's own claims:** "The Estimize consensus has proven more accurate than comparable sell side data sets **over 70% of the time and by 15% on average**" ([about page](https://www.estimize.com/about)); their FAQ states "**Estimize is more accurate than Wall Street 74% of the time**" ([FAQ](https://www.estimize.com/faq)). Earlier marketing cited **65% when there are 20+ contributors**. These are vendor claims — treat the 65–74% range as the marketing envelope, not a measured constant. Scale: 130,000+ contributors, 3,000+ stocks, point-in-time history back to January 2012.

**The academic verdict — Jame, Johnston, Markov & Wolfe (2016), "The Value of Crowdsourced Earnings Forecasts", Journal of Accounting Research 54(4):1077–1110** ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1475-679X.12121), [SSRN 2333671](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2333671)). Sample: 51,012 Estimize forecasts. Findings: Estimize forecasts are **incrementally useful** in forecasting earnings and in measuring the market's expectation of earnings; results are **stronger when the number of contributors is larger** (consistent with crowd-size benefits); Estimize consensus revisions generate significant two-day size-adjusted returns. Note the careful framing — *incrementally useful*, i.e. the combination of Estimize + sell-side beats sell-side alone. It is **not** a claim that a crowd replaces analysts.

**What this calibrates for us:** a large, motivated, financially-incentivised crowd of 130k humans achieves something in the region of a 65–74% paired win rate against sell-side consensus. **That is the ceiling, not the floor.** An LLM agent built in one day getting 55–60% would be a genuinely good result. Anything above 75% on a small sample should be treated as a bug or leakage until proven otherwise — see §4.2 for exactly how easily 75% arises from noise at n=20.

### 6.2 LLMs predicting earnings

**Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev (2024), "Financial Statement Analysis with Large Language Models"** — [arXiv 2407.17866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866), Chicago Booth. The canonical result. Method: feed GPT-4 **standardised and anonymised** financial statements (no names, no dates, no narrative), with chain-of-thought mimicking an analyst's steps, and ask for the *direction* of future earnings change. Results:

- GPT-4 directional accuracy **60.35%**, vs **~7 percentage points better than analyst forecasts** measured one month after the earnings release.
- Accuracy **on par with a narrowly-trained state-of-the-art ML model** — i.e. the LLM matched a purpose-built classifier without task-specific training.
- The LLM's relative advantage is largest **exactly where analysts struggle**.
- Trading strategies on GPT's predictions yield **higher Sharpe ratios and alphas** than strategies on other models.
- They explicitly argue the prediction **does not stem from training memory**, on the strength of the anonymisation.

Two things to take from it: (i) **~60% directional accuracy is the credible published number** for a strong LLM on this family of tasks; (ii) their anonymisation was far more aggressive than masking a ticker — it was a numbers-only balance sheet and income statement.

**FinCall-Surprise** — [arXiv 2510.03965](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.03965), ACL 2026. First large-scale open-source **multimodal** earnings-surprise benchmark: **2,688 conference calls, 2019–2021**, with word-for-word transcripts, full audio, and presentation slides. 26 unimodal and multimodal LLMs benchmarked. [Dataset](https://github.com/Tizzzzy/FinCall-Surprise). Three findings that matter to us:
1. **"While many models achieve high accuracy, this performance is often an illusion caused by significant class imbalance in the real-world data."** — This is the same trap as §3.2: ~75% of companies beat, so "always predict beat" scores 75%. **Our metric must be error-based and paired, not raw directional accuracy.**
2. Specialised financial models show unexpected weaknesses in instruction-following.
3. Audio and visual modalities give only marginal gains; models can't yet use them well.

Note the 2019–2021 window is deep inside every current model's training data — **FinCall-Surprise scores are not clean** and should be read as an upper bound.

**Look-Ahead-Bench** — [arXiv 2601.13770](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.13770), [code](https://github.com/benstaf/lookaheadbench). Evaluates agents in a realistic trading workflow rather than Q&A, using the AI Hedge Fund framework. Metric: **alpha decay** between in-sample and out-of-sample windows. Standard LLMs: alpha decay worse than **−15pp**. Directly borrowable as our "how leaky are we?" chart.

**Profit Mirage / FinLake-Bench / FactFin** — [arXiv 2510.07920](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07920). Leakage-robust benchmark plus a counterfactual-perturbation training framework. Key claim again: **most published LLM financial agents fail to beat baselines once past their knowledge cutoff.**

**No Kaggle competition on earnings surprise** turned up that is both current and well-specified enough to use as a benchmark. The closest usable public artifacts are FinCall-Surprise (above) and the SEC's own XBRL data. Not a gap worth spending hackathon time on.

### 6.3 The naive baselines, quantified

- **Seasonal random walk** (`E[Q_t] = Q_{t−4} + δ`) is the textbook SUE expectation model ([def.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earnings_surprise)). It is materially worse than analyst consensus for most large caps but is the honest floor for "no information".
- **Analyst consensus absolute error, EPS**: median **2–3 cents**, mean **5–6 cents**, IQR **4–5 cents**, essentially constant across share-price deciles (Cheong & Thomas 2010, [PDF](https://raw.rutgers.edu/docs/seminars/Cheong_Thomas_2010.pdf)). For longer-horizon annual forecasts, Gleason et al. report mean unscaled AFE **$0.30**, median **$0.13**, AFE/price **1.16%**, AFE/|actual EPS| **27.28%** ([PDF](https://care-mendoza.nd.edu/assets/152422/gleason_6_06.pdf)) — note that 27% figure is precisely the near-zero-denominator pathology from §3.4 showing up in the data.
- **Analyst consensus revenue error, aggregate**: **+1.6% to +1.9%** on average (FactSet 5/10-yr averages).
- **Beat rates**: EPS 76–78%, revenue 68–70% (FactSet 5/10-yr averages).

### 6.4 What win-rate is realistically achievable

No paper I found publishes a clean "% of quarters an independent model beat sell-side consensus on absolute error" for a general universe — the literature reports *incremental* usefulness (Jame et al.) or *directional* accuracy (Kim et al.), not paired absolute-error win rates. So the honest calibration is triangulated:

- Estimize's 130k-person crowd: **~65–74%** (vendor-claimed).
- GPT-4 directional accuracy on anonymised statements: **60.35%** (published).
- Baseline `consensus × (1+k)`, exploiting the documented 76% beat rate: mechanically **>50%**, plausibly 55–60%.

**Working target for tomorrow: 56–62% paired win rate on revenue, 52–58% on EPS, on ≥100 clean events.** If we hit that, we have a real result. If DEV shows 75%+, look for the leak before celebrating.

### C. Metrics (pre-register at 09:30, freeze)

- **Primary:** `SCORE = 0.5 × mean RSS(revenue) + 0.5 × mean RSS(EPS)`, where `RSS = (|err_cons| − |err_ours|) / (|err_cons| + |err_ours|)` ∈ [−1, 1].
- **Primary binary:** Paired Win Rate with tie bands **0.5¢ (EPS)** and **0.1% (revenue)**.
- **Revenue guardrail:** median APE. **EPS guardrail:** median absolute error **in cents** (target: beat 2–3¢), plus `|err|/price × 100`.
- **Never** MAPE or sMAPE on EPS.
- **Always** report: coverage (refusals = losses), directional accuracy, signed bias, cost/event, latency/event, PWR by sector.

### F. Statistical honesty (put this on a slide)

At n=40, we need **26/40 (65%)** to reject at α=0.05 one-sided — and if we try 20 configs on 40 events, **56% of the time one will hit 65% by pure chance**. At n=20 with 20 configs that rises to **94%**. Therefore: **grow the eval set rather than the config count**, report DEV and TEST separately, and quote the TEST number with its bootstrap CI as the result. A defensible 58% on 160 held-out events beats an indefensible 75% on 20.
